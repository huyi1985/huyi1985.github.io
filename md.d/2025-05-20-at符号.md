**Unexpected historical resonance behind Python’s choice of `@` for matrix multiplication**

**Historical Echoes in Python’s @ Operator(Matrix Multiplication)**

PEP 465 introduced the `@` operator for matrix multiplication in Python, and includes a section on why `@` was chosen over other alternatives ([PEP 465 – Choice of operator](https://peps.python.org/pep-0465/#choice-of-operator)). The rationale mentions that "`@` resembles a swirl, evocative of sweeping over rows and columns," along with a playful nod to "mATrices."

But one historical echo not noted in the PEP is that the `@` symbol was used as far back as a few hundred years ago(14th–16th centuries) to mean “at a rate of” or “per unit” — for example, “10 widgets @ £1.50 each.” (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_sign#History)

In that context, `@` implicitly signifies multiplication: `unit × quantity = total`. While scalar multiplication is very different from matrix multiplication, the underlying idea of combining values multiplicatively is unexpectedly resonant.

Maybe `@` was an even more fitting choice than originally thought.

**为什么 Python 用 `@` 表示矩阵乘法？其实早在几百年前就埋下伏笔了……**

Python 从 3.5 开始，引入了 `@` 符号来表示矩阵乘法，这是通过 PEP 465 提案确定的。文档中提到选择 `@` 的理由，比如这个符号像一股旋涡，形象地表现出矩阵运算中“行与列交错扫过”的感觉；还有一个小彩蛋：它刚好在“mATrices”这个词中。

但文档里**没提到的一个历史巧合**是：早在 14～16 世纪，`@` 就已经被用来表示“单价”或“每单位”，比如 “10 个零件 @ £1.50”，意思是“每个 £1.50”。  
👉 来源：[维基百科 - @ 的历史](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_sign#History)

这种用法其实**就暗含了乘法的意思**：单价 × 数量 = 总价。

虽然标量乘法和矩阵乘法是完全不同的数学结构，但“用乘法组合数值”的核心思想却意外地一致。  
也许 Python 当初选 `@`，比我们想的还更贴切？
## # Why is @ on your computer keyboard?

[](https://www.youtube.com/@InkboxSoftware)

Why is "@" this symbol, the at-sign, on your keyboard?

第一封邮件从这里发出
You may have heard the popularized story where, **in 1971 Ray Tomlinson** was looking for a way to distinguish(split? delimit?) between users and hosts in his new designs a networked electronic mail system, a precursor to email.

And while this wasn't the first case of sending messages between computers, Ray was the first person to use the @ symbol to designate where the email was to go. E.G. `user@host.com`.

Many contemporary sources have repeatedly claimed that the @ sign was almost doomed to be cast off from the keyboard due to its little use, if it wasn't saved by the likes of Ray Tomlinson.

But I feel like that story leaves too many holes, why was there an @ his keyboard to
choose from anyways? And what was its function in computing up until then?

Though perhaps it is best explained by first understanding the origin of the @, where did
it come from and what was its primordial function?

At the heart of the @, is quite literally, an A.

The **earliest** recorded document containing something familiar to the @ known to us today comes from the Vatican archives.

In this Bulgarian translation of a Greek chronicle written by Constantinos Manasses in 1345, he ends his paper with the word "Amen" featuring this stylized A.

The Mediterranean world began the use the circled A in a more symbolic sense.

In this 1448 letter from the Kingdom of Castile to the Kingdom of Aragon, and this letter
from the Florentine merchant Francesco Lapiun, the @ symbol is used to represent a **unit of weight known as an "arroba (الربع )"**, a term borrowed from the Arabs that has been around since at least 1088 and which was in popular use until the 19th century.

And as @ would begin to spread around continental Europe it also took on new meanings.

As seen in France, where it was originally used in the same sense of **arroba**, and by the
17th century came to represent **the price of a unit of something**.

As in 1 bottle of wine @ 4 ducats. So this was how the stylized A became "at",
at least in reference to the commercial market, and this use spread over from France to Sweden and England.

And even today this symbol is still known as the "commercial at" in the English speaking
world.

Also note that this stylized A also held different meanings throughout Europe, as in 1550’s Sudebnik of Ivan IV, a collection of laws laid out by the Russian monarch, which uses the **@ to signify point one**.

Or in Italy and Germany where @ was used to represent “versus” in court cases.

But these other meanings never found their way into English.

Now up until this point in time in the West, the written word was, written, hand written.

But that was all changing with the spread of the Gutenberg Printing Press throughout
Europe.

Letters and other symbols, which were until this point written more or less according
to tradition rather than rule, suddenly had to become concrete or rather lead, in their
form.

**In a sense, one could call this the first encoding of a language, taking all these forms of a letter, and representing it in a physical "code" so to speak.**

And yes, @ does show up in these new typefaces created by printers.

It was physically encoded not only in type, but it also made its way onto commercial typewriters in the late 1800's as well.

Even before the typewriter keyboard had been standardized to the QWERTY we English speakers know today, it had already made itself at home, usually sitting at the edge with some other commercial key like the cent sign.

And as electrical communications, like the telegraph and later teletype, grew in popularity so did another phenomenon of the soon to be modern world, the need to store and process the vast amounts of data that were being produced.

The Constitution of the United States mandates that a census must be conducted every 10 years to apportion congressional representation, and as the nation grew the time it took to process all the data for the decennial census kept growing alongside it.

The 1880 census, for example, wasn't completed for 7 years. Insert Herman Hollerith, a man who had seen punch cards used by train conductors to represent the data of passengers on tickets and thought that the system could also work for general information as well.

So he invented the **Electric Tabulating machine**, which ran on **Hollerith cards**, punch cards that hold a form of binary data, as each cell can be either punched or not punched.

This machine greatly reduced the amount of time needed to complete the 1890 census, and Hollerith went on to continue to develop his Tabulating machines.

In 1911, **Hollerith's company, along with four other similar data processing companies**, were amalgamated in New York forming the new Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, which later renamed itself in 1924 to **International Business Machines, IBM**.

During the succeeding years IBM would continue to use Hollerith Punch Cards, which came to be called IBM cards, to process data.

And by 1932 IBM expanded the original code devised by **Hollerith** into a set of 39 graphics(glyph?) encoded in **Binary Coded Decimal Interchange Code (BCDIC)**, **with a notable absence of @**.

These Electric Data Processors were the precursors of the first general purpose computers, the ENIAC, BINAC, and UNIVAC all of which used IBM cards to store data.

But in a time before screens and digital graphical output these computers would use Teletypes as console output, and eventually input as well.

And look at the teletypes these computers used. Look right there, the @ key. Now this @ key couldn't be used for actual data input into these computers because it wasn't encoded in BCDIC.

That was **until IBM cemented @ as part of computing history when in 1953** BCDIC was expanded to include 8 new characters, taken from these teletype keyboards, including the @.

Now with a proper encoding @ began to be used not just as a commercial symbol, but within different aspects of computing as well.

While IBM's FORTAN of 1953 didn't include @ as a keyword, it was used, along with 4
other characters, to represent symbols in the programming language that weren't encoded yet. @ was used here to represent the single quote.

And later in 1960 it was properly included in the LISP programming language to preform splice operations.

In 1963 the American Standards Association published their unified encoding scheme for the manufacturer, consumer, and general public. This encoding was called the American Standard Code for Information Interchange, ASCII, and right at position 1000000, sits @.

From this point on ASCII is included in almost every computer made and by extension @ becomes more popular.

@ was picked up by programming languages of that decade and even today is still frequently used in a variety of contexts.

So when Ray Tomlinson went to search for a symbol on his keyboard that day, it wasn't
a stroke of genius, and he didn't save @ from eradication.

The reason it was available for him to choose in the first place was because of its inclusion in ASCII.

So @ would be have been on your computer keyboard today regardless of whether email used it or not.

It was a part of ASCII, BCDIC, and was integral
to keyboards and typists since near the beginning.
It's the character with perhaps the richest
and most unique history on the keyboard.
@, a strange character for a strange world.
